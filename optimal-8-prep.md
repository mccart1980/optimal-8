# OPTIMAL 8 · PREP — FOURTEEN WEEKS TO THE CAMP

**The block between the program and the camp: fourteen weeks at its longest, always ending on test day — this year ten, from Monday 26 October to test day on Saturday 2 January — then the camp from Monday 4 January to the fight on Saturday 13 March. Its only job: hand the camp a body that is already at the camp's week-3 level on the camp's first day — stronger, more explosive, aerobically deeper, and moving better than it has in years.**

Optimal 8 Fighter's sessions, your calisthenics, RANGE, the tendon block, Iron Mind underneath — reorganised into three blocks with one job each instead of fourteen weeks of the same week, loaded by the bar speed on your wrist instead of by percentages of a stale number, gated each morning by the numbers on the strap, with the engine progressed on the erg readings, and with the things you asked for built in where they belong: a movement session twice a week through the build and once a week after, and the strength and the look of your upper body — an upper-body day on Monday, weighted dips and the bench press every week, three circuits and three carries — because Prep is the everyday program, and the camp keeps what it builds.

**The three blocks.** Weeks 1–5 ACCUMULATE — volume, base, tissue, size, movement; week 5 lighter, with tests and the working weights re-checked. Weeks 6–10 INTENSIFY — heavy, paused then fast, the top triples in week 9, week 10 easy with tests. Weeks 11–14 CONVERT — contrast, reactive jumps, top speed, repeat bursts, rounds at fight rest, and the test week at the end. Then the camp.

**Honest ceiling, stated up front.** Against running Optimal 8 as it stands, this adds perhaps five to ten percent to where the camp starts and a materially better aerobic base. It is not magic. The best programs are simple and executed without a missed sleep, a skipped feed or a fudged easy week; the program is a third of the result.

## LOADING — THE BAR SPEED IS THE LAW

The calendar gives percentages because you need a number to load the bar with. The watch decides whether the number was right. Three rules, and they replace the fast-bar rule for the whole preparation.

**1 · The first work set sets the day.** Every main lift has a velocity target for its phase, from your own profile (below). Load the bar to the calendar's percentage and do the first work set. Read the average of its two fastest reps. Within 0.05 m/s of target: stay. More than 0.05 slower: take 5% off for the remaining sets — one slow reading is enough to come down. More than 0.05 faster: stay at today's load; if the same lift comes up more than 0.05 fast again next session, add 2.5% from then on. Coming down on one reading is safe; going up on one reading is chasing the watch's noise. Same setup every time — the watch on the same wrist, the same depth, the same tempo — or the numbers don't compare. And hold the lockout, still, until Spleeft beeps before the next rep: it tells one rep from the next by that stop, so there's no need to rack the bar between reps.

**2 · The set ends at the threshold.** Strength sets: the set is over when a rep is 20% slower than the set's first rep. Fast and contrast sets: 10%. The watch alerts; you rack it. Rep counts are ceilings.

**3 · No true maxes, ever.** The heaviest work is a top triple — a set of three that's hard but leaves one or two reps in you — in week 9 and in test week, on pins, after the warm-up ramp. That weight × 1.08 resets the working max. For a fighter a one-rep max is only a number to set loads with; the top triple and your bar-speed profile set them without the risk of failing a lift alone.

**The pause squat runs off its percentage alone.** The two-second pause makes its bar speed read slow, so the watch doesn't judge it; the calendar's percentage of the squat's working max is the load, and it never goes above 75%.

**The re-check, week 5.** On the lighter week, each main lift gets a working-weight check before its working sets — the trap bar and the bench on Wednesday, the squat and the push press on Saturday: a set of 3 that's hard but leaves two in you, × 1.08, rounded to 2.5 kg. The app saves it, and the week's sets read off it straight away. Coming back from time off, strength climbs faster than the bar-speed steps can follow; this catches it up.

**Your profile, week 1.** On Wednesday of week 1, after the sled: trap bar at five loads — 50, 60, 70, 80, 85% of your current working max — two reps each, fast, the watch recording. On Saturday, the squat the same way, then bench and push press at four loads. Spleeft draws the line for each lift; that line is what the targets below read off. Redraw the profiles in week 10 after the top triples. The numbers below are typical; yours replace them the day the profile is drawn.

**The ballistic loads.** The bench throw and the trap bar jump are loaded for power, not by feel. In the week-1 and week-10 profiles, do both at four loads — the bench throw at about 20, 30, 40 and 50% of your bench, the trap bar jump at 10, 20, 30 and 40% of the trap bar working weight — two reps each, the watch recording. Power is load times speed: the load with the highest number is the one you use until the next profile.

## READINESS — THE STRAP GATES THE DAY

On waking, lying still: one minute on the strap, resting heart rate and HRV into the app. Then the four questions. The colour decides the day, and in this preparation the numbers can turn a day yellow on their own:

- **GREEN** — questions 0–1 yes, heart rate within 5 of baseline, HRV within 12% of its seven-day average. Train as written.
- **YELLOW** — 2–3 yes, OR heart rate 5+ over, OR HRV 12%+ below its average. 7% off the bar, the interval session at 90%, skip the last block. The velocity rule will usually have said the same thing by the first work set.
- **RED** — 4 yes, ill, injured, three yellows running, OR heart rate 8+ over AND HRV 15%+ below on the same morning. Warm-up, neck, hands, RANGE, home.

Two yellows in a row on HRV alone: Tuesday's or Thursday's hard session becomes the easy 20 minutes and the base is unchanged. Recalibrate the baselines at the start of weeks 6 and 11.

**The jump check — Tuesday.** Three broad jumps after the warm-up, measured with a tape on the floor, the best distance logged. More than 5% under your four-week average counts as a yellow. It reads tired legs and a tired nervous system, which the strap can't.

**The tendon gate — Sunday.** Achilles or knee at 3 or more out of 10 on the weekly check: no reactive jumps the following week — no drop landings, no depth jumps — while the Spanish squat hold and the Achilles hold carry on, because holds are what a sore tendon needs. Hamstring at 3 or more: no flying sprints the following week, and the Nordics only if they're pain-free. When it's back under 3, the jumps restart one stage down for a week (depth jumps → low-box depth jumps → drop landings). And at any time: a jump, sprint or hold that hurts more than 3 out of 10 while you're doing it, or leaves the tendon worse the next morning, stops for that day. Still there after two weeks: a physio looks at it. The gate runs separately from the colours, because a sore tendon doesn't show on HRV.

## THE ENGINE — PROGRESSED ON THE ERG, NOT ON FEEL

Every conditioning session has a number from last time, and the number is the target.

**How to log one:** nothing during it. Zero the machine's screen as the timer starts and keep moving through the easy parts; when the timer says done, log the total on the screen and your peak heart rate. The app shows last time's beside it.

- **Base (Monday, the easy hour):** the average heart rate stays in 65–75% of peak; the output at that heart rate is what should rise. Log both. A higher output at the same heart rate is the base getting deeper — that's the whole point of five weeks of it. Judge it on a four-week trend, never one ride: morning heart rate moves three to five beats with sleep, heat and stress, and that swamps a week's change. Up across each four weeks is right. Flat for four weeks: check the sleep line and the yellow days first; if they're clean, hold the minutes and keep it nasal.
- **4-minute intervals:** the session total; beat it by 1–2% each time, never by more.
- **40-second repeats:** the session total. Hold the last repeats near the first and it climbs; the burst test in the test weeks measures the decrement itself.
- **Repeat bursts:** the session total. Same.
- **Threshold:** the session total for the two eights.
- **Rounds:** the fade — round six over round one — scored in weeks 2, 10 and 13. From 90-second rest in accumulation to 60 from week 6. Rounds past the fight are capacity work, not punishment: if one drops below 75% of round one's output, or your posture goes, that round is the last.
- **The 20-minute test:** weeks 1, 5 and 14. Distance and peak heart rate.
- **Recovery heart rate:** the drop at 60 seconds on every settle. It should grow.

PROGRESS charts every one of them, week against week, and the camp inherits them as its week-1 starting line.

## MONDAY · UPPER STRENGTH + BASE · ~77 min

The low day for your legs and your engine, and the second heavy day for your upper body. The dips and the rows go first, while you're fresh; then the arms circuit; then the base, easy — the floor that lets Tuesday and Thursday be as hard as they are. Strength rises with how often a lift is trained, and size with how many hard sets a muscle gets a week: Monday adds both.

**1 · 0:00 — WARM-UP · 4 min** — band pull-aparts × 20 → band external rotations × 15 per arm → ring support hold, 2 × 20 seconds: arms locked at the top of the rings, the rings dead still by your hips, shoulders pushed down → dips at bodyweight × 5.

**2 · 0:04 — WEIGHTED DIPS + RING ROWS · rounds from the calendar · 60 s after each · 12 min**

Weighted dips: parallel bars, weight on a belt. Lower until your shoulders are level with your elbows, chest slightly forward, then press back up to locked arms. No deeper: your shoulders are stiff, and that's the depth where they're safe. Rest 60 seconds. Then the ring rows at your level: rings at hip height, body straight, heels on the floor, pull the rings to your chest, pause, lower slowly (Calisthenics page). Rest 60 seconds, then the next round. Four rounds in the build and heavy weeks, three in weeks 5, 9 and 11–13, two in weeks 10 and 14. The app's timer calls every set.

The dips by block: **weeks 1–5, 6–8 reps** — leave two in you every set; when every set reaches 8, add 2.5 kg next week. **Weeks 6–9, 4–6 reps, heavier** — when every set reaches 6, add 2.5 kg. **Weeks 11–13, 4–5 reps, driven up fast** — the set ends the moment a rep slows. **Weeks 10 and 14, 5 reps, light and fast** — a weight you could do ten with. Log the weight on the belt and the reps in the last round. Weighted dips are one of the best builders of chest and triceps there is, and the pressing strength carries straight to the bench; the rows balance them, pulling the shoulder back where punching pulls it forward.

**3 · 0:16 — THE ARMS CIRCUIT · rounds from the calendar · 13 min**

Back to back, 20 seconds between exercises, 90 seconds after each round. Leave two reps in you on each; when every round reaches the top of the range, go up a dumbbell next time. Three rounds in the build and heavy weeks, two in weeks 5, 9 and 11–13, one in week 10, none in week 14.

- **Incline dumbbell curl × 10–12** — lie back on a bench set at about 45°, arms hanging straight down behind you. Curl without the elbows moving forward, then lower all the way to a full stretch.
- **Overhead triceps extension × 10–12** — a rope on a high cable facing away, or one dumbbell held in both hands. Elbows pointing at the ceiling, lower behind your head to a full stretch, then extend. The triceps are most of the upper arm, and the stretched position grows them best.
- **Lateral raise × 15** — a dumbbell in each hand at your sides; raise them out to shoulder height, elbows leading, and lower slowly.
- **Rear-delt fly × 15** — bent over flat, small dumbbells out to the sides, little fingers leading.

Circuits build the same muscle as straight sets in far less time, which is how the extra work fits — and they train shoulders and arms that keep working when they're tired, which is a non-stop puncher's sixth round.

**4 · 0:29 — EASY, NOSE ONLY · 40 min**

Bike, SkiErg, or a run once your legs and ankles are used to it — a run builds the base faster but costs more. Heart rate on the strap and the average logged: 65–75% of the peak heart rate from the 20-minute test, no higher. The output at that heart rate is the number that should climb. Nose the whole way; if you can't hold a sentence, slow down. Forty minutes every week, after the upper-body work. Boring on purpose. Fine on an empty stomach.

**5 · 1:11 — HANDS · 4 min**

Knuckle hold, 3 × 20 seconds — push-up position on your fists, wrist dead straight. Band wrist extension, 2 × 15 — forearm on your knee, palm down, band in the hand, lift the knuckles toward you.

**6 · 1:15 — BAND DECELERATION CATCH · 2 × 8 per arm · 2 min**

A light band anchored behind you at shoulder height, the end in your hand, in your stance. Punch the arm out fast; the band snatches it back — brake it hard and stop it dead in the last third of the return. Fast out, hard stop. Every punch you throw has to be braked and brought home by these muscles, and this is the only exercise that trains them to do it.

**An elbow or a shoulder at 4 or more on Sunday's check:** next week the dips become push-ups, 12–15 with two seconds down, the rows are held at the top for 20 seconds, and the circuits drop their curls and triceps work. The neck has moved to Wednesday and Thursday.

**Done · ~1:17.** Tonight: RANGE, the skill block, the sit.

## TUESDAY · POWER + ENGINE 1 · ~68 min

Fresh legs, so the power dose goes first. Then the week's first hard conditioning session. Then THE BRACE, because the trunk is what turns leg drive into hand speed and takes the shots to the body.

**1 · 0:00 — WARM-UP · 7 min**

3 easy minutes on the bike → band pull-aparts × 20 → goblet squats × 8 → push-ups × 10 → 90/90 hip switches × 5 each way → pogo hops × 20.

**THE JUMP CHECK · 1 min** — three broad jumps. Lay a tape measure along the floor, its end on a line and a plate on each end. Toes behind the line, swing your arms, jump as far forward as you can and land on both feet, dead still. Read the tape at the back of the heel nearer the line — fall back or step and it doesn't count. Walk back, reset, go again, and log the best in centimetres. A tape, not a phone: an app on your arm can't time a jump steadily enough for this rule. More than 5% under your four-week average counts as a yellow (the Readiness page) — it reads tired legs and a tired nervous system, which the strap can't.

**2 · 0:08 — POWER DOSE · JUMPS · 8 min · rest 90 s**

Broad jumps, 3 × 2 — two-foot jump forward for distance, stick the landing dead still. Box jumps, 3 × 3 — weeks 11–13: 4 × 3 — the box chosen by the landing: you land on it in a quarter squat. Quick dip, jump as high as you can, land soft, step down. Explosiveness responds to how often the nervous system is asked, not how much. Eight minutes, three mornings a week.

**SHUTTLE BURSTS — from week 11:** six times five metres out and back, a hard push-off at each turn, twenty seconds between. A mover with good feet makes you change direction; the side bounds train it sideways, this trains it forward and back. Three minutes.

**SPEED — from week 11, after the shuttles:** one 20-metre build-up at 75%, one at 90%, then three flying 20-metre sprints, absolutely flat out, rest 2½ minutes. Curved treadmill or outdoors. Twice a week is what sprinters do, and it's the best hamstring insurance there is — provided the build-ups never miss. Eight minutes.

**3 · 0:16 — THE PISTOL LINE · 2 × 5 per leg at your level · rest 60 s · 5 min**

Box pistol to start — stand on one leg in front of a box, the other straight out in front, sit to the box under control and stand without the free foot touching — climbing to the assisted and then the full pistol (Calisthenics page). Two sets, never to failure: control, not load. The pivot foot learning to own the body.

**SPANISH SQUAT HOLD · 3 × 30 seconds · rest 30 s · 3 min** — as Thursday. The tendon block runs twice a week now, because the jumps do.

**4 · 0:24 — ENGINE 1 · 24 min · the table says which**

Bike or SkiErg — one, all camp, so the numbers compare. Nothing to log during the session: at the end, the total on the machine's screen and your peak heart rate.

Moderate intervals (week 1): 4 × 3 minutes at a pace where you could speak short sentences, 3 easy between. The engine measured before it's pushed.

4-minute intervals: 4 × 4 minutes HARD — breathing heavily, two or three words at most — 3 easy between. The best-proven builder of the aerobic ceiling there is.

Repeat bursts: 8 bursts of 6–8 seconds at absolute maximum with 40 seconds easy between; 5 full minutes easy; 8 more. This is a flurry, twenty or thirty seconds apart, for a round — and what decides whether the fourth flurry has anything in it is how fast the muscle refills between them. If a burst is visibly weaker than the last, take an extra 20 seconds; if two in a row are, the set is over.

40-second repeats (the weeks the calendar puts them on Tuesday): as Thursday's page.

Easy (weeks 5, 10 and 14): 20 minutes conversational.

Every session ends with **the 60-second settle**: stay on the bike, eyes closed, heart pounding, find the breath at the nostrils, count the seconds it takes to land there. The corner between rounds, trained.

**5 · 0:49 — THE BRACE · rounds from the calendar · 13 min**

The body-shot block. Back to back, 20 seconds between exercises, 60 seconds after each round. Three rounds in the build and heavy weeks, two in weeks 5, 9 and 11–13, one in week 10, none in week 14.

- **Ab wheel rollout × 8–12** — knees down; roll out only as far as your lower back stays flat, then pull back.
- **Pallof press × 10 per side** — a band at chest height anchored beside you; press your hands straight out and hold 2 seconds without letting it twist you.
- **Medicine-ball drops × 8** — lie on your back, knees bent, a 3 kg ball held at arm's length over your stomach. Breathe in, then let it go and breathe out hard through your teeth as you brace. It lands on the brace just above your belly button; catch it on the bounce. Drops 5 to 8 land a hand's width to each side, alternating. Never on the ribs. Go up a kilo when all eight land on a brace that doesn't give, from 3 kg up to 6.
- **Copenhagen plank · 25 seconds per side** — side plank, top foot on a bench, bottom leg lifted: the groin you pivot off. Rounds 1 and 2 only.

Body-shot toughness comes down to three things: the brace firing on time, the muscle wall that takes the shot, and the nerves. The drops train the timing and settle the nerves a little at a time; the ab wheel, the Pallof press, the Copenhagen plank and the carries build the wall. No study proves the drops; they're low-risk, and they're the first rung. Being hit to the body for real is your coach's job, when sparring starts.

## THE MOVEMENT SESSION · 30 min · Wednesday and Saturday evenings in weeks 1–5, Saturday evenings from week 6

You named movement and flexibility as the thing holding you back. RANGE is the daily dose — long holds and end-range strength on the two joints you named. This is the second dose, and it's different: it moves the whole body through its range under control, slowly, so the range RANGE finds becomes range you can use. Nothing here is a strength session. Nothing here is rushed. Kit: the kettlebell, a stick or broom handle, the band, the floor.

## THE HOME BLOCK · THE MORNING FIVE · RANGE · THE SKILL BLOCK · THE SIT

Two joints you named as your limiting factor: hips and shoulders. Both are limited first by the mid-back, so it goes first. Eight minutes a night was maintenance; this is change. Twenty minutes every evening for all fourteen weeks, five on waking, and the tests in weeks 1, 5, 10 and 14. You named this as the thing holding you back; this is the daily dose, and the Movement Session on its own page is the second. Kit: a foam roller, a light band, a kettlebell or dumbbell (8–16 kg), a doorframe pull-up bar if you can fit one, a sofa and a chair.

**How range changes — so you do it right.** Stiffness is partly the tissue and mostly the nervous system guarding it. Three things move it, and the eight-minute block did none of them: long holds — ninety seconds to three minutes, breathing slowly out through the nose, because a long exhale is what tells the body it's safe to let go; contract-relax — pushing gently into the stretch for five seconds at a third of your strength, then relaxing and sinking further, three times, which resets the guard directly; and strength at the end of the range, because the body only keeps range it can control. Every joint below gets all three. Never into pain; into a strong stretch, and stay.

**One line on the hips, worth reading twice.** A stretch should pull at the back of the hip, the groin or the front of the thigh. If a deep squat or the 90/90 *pinches* at the front of the hip, or one side is markedly worse than the other, that's a joint pattern, not a muscle one — get it looked at before the twelve weeks, and we work around it.

### THE MORNING FIVE · on waking, after the sighs and the one thing · 5 min

Controlled joint circles — the slowest, biggest circle each joint can make under its own power, three each way. Slow enough that it takes ten seconds a circle; if something wants to cheat — a shrug, a lean, a bent knee — that's the range you don't own yet, so slow down there.

Neck: three circles each way, gentle, chin leading. Shoulders: standing, arm straight, palm turned as needed, the arm draws the biggest circle it can — forward, up past the ear, back and down — three each way, the trunk dead still, no shrug. Mid-back: cat-camel × 8, then on all fours, hand behind head, elbow to the ceiling × 6 each side. Hips: hold a wall, lift the knee to the chest, out to the side, round to the back and down — three each way, standing tall. Ankles: circles × 5 each way, then 10 slow deep knee-bends over the toes with the heels down.

### RANGE · every evening · 20 min · twelve weeks, then the keep

**0 · Down-regulate · 1 min** — lie on your back, feet on a chair, five slow breaths: in through the nose for 4, out for 8. Range work on a wound-up nervous system is wasted.

**1 · MID-BACK · 5 min — first, always**

Foam roller extensions — roller across the mid-back, hands behind head, arch back over it × 8, moving up a notch each time; then find the stiffest notch and stay there, 45 seconds, breathing out.

Open book, 90 seconds per side — lie on your side, knees bent and stacked, arms together out front; open the top arm to the far side, chest following, knees glued down. Hold 3 seconds at the end, come back, again. Contract-relax: at the end of the range, press the top hand gently *toward the ceiling* against nothing for 5 seconds, relax, open further.

Thread the needle × 8 per side — on all fours, slide one arm under the other, shoulder toward the floor, hold 3 seconds.

Loaded rotation × 6 per side — side-lying windmill: on your side, knees bent, a light dumbbell in the top hand held straight up; keeping the knees down, arc the weight over to the far side and back. The weight is what makes the range yours.

**2 · HIPS · 8 min**

90/90, 2 minutes per side — sit with the front leg bent at a right angle in front of you, the back leg bent at a right angle behind, both sitting bones on the floor. First 60 seconds: fold the chest over the front shin, breathing out — that's external rotation. Contract-relax: press the front shin into the floor for 5 seconds, relax, fold further, three times. Then the internal-rotation lift, 8 reps: sit tall, and lift the back foot off the floor without leaning, hold 3 seconds, lower. It'll barely move at first. This lift is the single most important movement on this page for a hook and a cross — it's the hip learning to turn under control.

Couch stretch, 90 seconds per side — back foot up on the sofa behind you, knee down on a cushion, front foot forward, squeeze the glute, stand tall. Contract-relax: drive the back knee into the cushion for 5 seconds, relax, stand taller.

Cossack squats × 6 per side, 3-second pause at the bottom — feet very wide, sit all the way down over one leg, the other straight with the heel down. The loaded end-range for the groin.

Deep squat hold, 90 seconds — a weight at the chest, sit in the very bottom, elbows prying the knees out, heels down, breathing out. Then squat-to-stand × 5 — from standing, fold and hold your toes, pull down into the full squat with the chest up, then straighten the legs while keeping hold of the toes, and back down.

Hip airplanes × 5 per side — stand on one leg holding a chair, hinge the chest to horizontal, rotate the chest toward the floor and then up to the ceiling. Active control at the end of the range.

**3 · SHOULDERS · 6 min**

Hang, 60–90 seconds — from a doorframe bar, feet on the floor as much as you need, shoulders relaxed up by the ears, breathing out. No bar: hands on a table, step back, hinge and let the chest sink between the arms, 90 seconds.

Prayer stretch, 90 seconds — kneel in front of a chair, elbows on the seat, hands behind your head, sink the chest toward the floor. Contract-relax: press the elbows into the seat for 5 seconds, relax, sink. Overhead range, and the lats that steal it.

Sleeper stretch, 60 seconds per side — lie on your side, bottom arm straight out in front bent at a right angle, forearm pointing at the ceiling; with the other hand press the forearm gently toward the floor. Internal rotation, the range that keeps a guard from riding up. Contract-relax: push the forearm up into your hand for 5 seconds, relax, press further.

Kettlebell arm bar, 45 seconds per side — lie on your back, a light kettlebell pressed up in the right hand, right knee bent; roll onto your left side and slowly straighten the right leg behind you until you're lying on your front with the bell still pointing at the ceiling, arm straight, shoulder packed. Breathe there. The loaded end-range for the shoulder, and the best opener there is for a chest and mid-back that have closed.

Wall slides × 12, with a 3-second reach at the top — back flat on the wall, forearms on the wall like a goalpost, slide up without them leaving the wall. Band external rotations × 15 per arm. The range you just found, put under control.

**Test week (week 14):** RANGE as normal — a light week is not a stiff one.

### THE FOUR RANGE TESTS · weeks 1, 5, 10 and 14 · on the Sunday weekly check · plus the three on the Movement page

1. **90/90 sit** — both sitting bones on the floor, no hands: yes or no each side, and how many centimetres the back knee sits off the floor.
2. **Deep squat** — heels down, 60 seconds: heels stayed down, thighs below parallel, back flat — three yes-or-nos.
3. **Wall flexion** — back to a wall, heels ten centimetres out, lower back and head on the wall, straight arms raised overhead: do the thumbs reach the wall, and if not, by how many centimetres.
4. **Hands behind the back** — one hand over the shoulder, the other up from below: the gap between the fingertips in centimetres, each way round.

Write the numbers down. If nothing has moved by week 5, the holds aren't long enough or the exhale isn't happening; fix that before adding anything.

Skill. ## THE EDGE — THE REINS OFF, AND THE ONE THING THAT PUTS THEM BACK

Five additions, on by default, that turn this from a program into a professional camp's density in the same hours. None of them is grinding volume; every one is more high-quality exposure at a recovery cost the monitoring can see.

1. **Speed twice a week** — three flying twenties on Tuesday after the shuttles, from week 11. Build-ups every time. The Nordics never miss.
2. **Cluster sets in the heavy block** — five sets of two-plus-two at 87–90%, twenty seconds on the pins between the pairs. Pins set; the velocity rule ends the set.
3. **More plyometric contacts** in weeks 11–13 — box jumps 4 × 3 and side bounds 4 × 4; depth jumps held at 4 × 4, because sixteen clean landings beat twenty-five tired ones. The tendon block runs twice a week, the tendon gate applies, and the output rule is law.
4. **Rounds past the fight** — seven in the convert block. Scored; the fade governs.
5. **The Thursday easy thirty** — a second base session, walk or run, nose only. Easy, or it comes out.

**THE GUARDRAIL.** Two yellow mornings on the strap in the same week — resting heart rate five over, or HRV twelve percent under — and the five come off for the rest of that week. Not the program: the additions. Tuesday's speed and the Thursday thirty go first; the clusters become straight sets; the contacts drop back; the rounds go back to six. The base program is what keeps you training. The edge is what makes you dangerous, and it's only worth having if you're standing on the day.

**THE TENDON GATE AND THE JUMP CHECK.** Separately from the strap: Achilles or knee at 3 or more on Sunday's check means no reactive jumps the following week, with the tendon holds carried on, and a hamstring at 3 or more means no flying sprints — a sore tendon doesn't show on HRV. And Tuesday's jump check more than 5% under its four-week average counts as a yellow, toward the two that take the Edge off.

## THE THINKING (read at home, not in the gym)

**What the October audit changed, and why.** Every major system was run back over this program — block periodization, conjugate, Sheiko, autoregulation, Easy Strength, triphasic, Charlie Francis, the shock method, velocity training, ALTIS and the deceleration research, Joel Jamieson, the HIIT and polarized research, the UFC Performance Institute and the boxing research. Out, because they weren't earning their minutes: Saturday's tired throws (Thursday and Sunday throw fresh), the evening microdoses (explosive work already runs five days a week), the warm-up crawls, Sunday's get-ups and the planche leans. In, because each fills a real gap: braking into your stance, strength in the punch position, loaded sideways trunk stiffness, a jump check that reads what the strap can't, the arm-braking work twice a week, the back of the shoulder all year, a bench press you're trained on before you're tested on it, and shrugs in the size weeks. Depth jumps came down to sixteen clean contacts, and a tendon gate went in. Sheiko's lesson was taken without Sheiko's volume: you get strong at what you practise, and you don't test a lift you don't train.

**What the October review changed, and why.** An outside review read the documents line by line and found four safety points worth taking. The dumbbell-release jumps went: dropped dumbbells bounce and roll, and you land where they fell — the reactive slot now climbs from drop landings to low-box depth jumps to full depth jumps. The bench throw now runs off the Smith machine's safety stops, so a missed catch lands on them, not on you. The true maxes went: for a fighter a one-rep max only sets loads, and the top triple with the bar-speed profile does that without the risk of failing a lift alone. And the tendon gate was fixed: a sore tendon gets its jumping taken away and its holds kept, not a different jump. Two rules tightened with them — the bar-speed rule now comes down on one slow reading but goes up only on two fast ones, because one reading is inside the watch's noise; and rounds past the fight stop if they fall below three-quarters of round one, because they're capacity work, not punishment.

**Why the six pressure-fighter additions.** You fight forward, non-stop, against men who are heavier, taller and mobile. That's four physical problems: crossing the distance a taller man keeps, again and again (stance starts, repeat sled starts, shuttle bursts); holding your ground when a heavier man leans on you (drive holds — an isometric, and the one quality the program had no exercise for); taking bigger punches (the neck at four sets of the rapid brace); and hitting up and in with more force at the same speed (the heavier ball, the landmine punch with the step-in). Fifteen minutes a week, nothing dropped, and none of it replaces what your coach does with distance, angles and the guard — it gives him a body that can get there, stay there and keep hitting.

**Why three blocks and not fourteen weeks of the same week.** A body adapts to what it's asked repeatedly, and it adapts fastest when the asking is concentrated: five weeks of volume and base builds tissue and depth that heavy work can't, five weeks of heavy work builds strength that volume can't, four weeks of contrast turns the strength into speed that neither can. Optimal 8 asks for all three every week and gets a little of each; this asks for one at a time and gets more of all three by January.

**Why the bar speed replaces the percentages.** A percentage is a guess about today from a number measured weeks ago. A velocity is a measurement of today. With the watch on your wrist the guess is no longer necessary, and the set that ends at a 20% slowdown is the set that built strength without building fatigue — that finding is as solid as anything in the field.

**Why the strap gates the day.** The daily check asks you how you feel; the strap tells you how you are. On a program built on 3:30 alarms and five hard days a week, the morning the two disagree is the morning that matters, and the numbers win.

**Why movement gets two sessions and a daily dose.** You said it holds you back, and for a boxer stiff hips and shoulders cost power directly — a hip that can't turn sends the force through the back and knee instead. RANGE finds the range with long holds and end-range strength; the movement session teaches the body to use it under control; the tests every five weeks make it a number. By the camp, this is done, and the camp doesn't have to spend a minute on it.

**What the October rebuild changed, and why.** Prep gave your shoulders, traps and chest roughly half the hard sets Optimal 8 Singles did: the bench didn't start until week 6, the dips stopped after week 5, and the camp had no size work at all. That suited a fighter-first brief, not one that asks for maximal strength, power and the look as well as the engine. So: Monday became an upper-body strength day — weighted dips paired with ring rows, then the arms circuit, then the base — because strength rises with how often a lift is trained and size with the hard sets a muscle gets a week. The dips run all year, weighted, on bars, so the weight can keep climbing. The bench press runs from week 1 in the trap bar's phases. Three circuits went in, because supersets and circuits build the same muscle as straight sets in far less time, and train shoulders and arms that keep working tired. Three carries went in for grip, traps and a trunk that stays stiff under load — proven by coaches more than by studies, and in because of what they train, cheaply. THE BRACE turned Tuesday's trunk work into the body-shot block. Lateral raises went into the trap bar's rest, a third shoulder dose at no cost in time. The one trade: Monday's base stays at 40 minutes instead of climbing to 60, because the upper-body work needs the time and the engine responds to training far faster than muscle does; the camp is engine-led, and that's where the engine is built to its peak. The neck goes to twice a week in Prep, Wednesday and Thursday; the camp keeps three. And the lighter week re-checks the working weights, because strength coming back after time off climbs faster than the bar-speed steps.

**What the leg fix changed, and why.** Set beside Optimal 8 Fighter, Prep was behind on one thing: leg strength. The Fighter squats twice a week — heavy on Saturday, a pause squat on Wednesday — and hinges with Romanian deadlifts; Prep squatted once, on Saturday, after the sprints, the jumps and the bounds had taken the best of your legs, and nothing loaded the hips and hamstrings through a long hinge. Strength rises with how often a lift is trained, so the pause squat went in on Wednesday after the trap bar, at a weight you own — 65–75%, two seconds at the bottom, a speed squat at 70% in the fast block — and the Romanian deadlift on Saturday after the push press, for the back of the legs. About fourteen minutes a week, and nothing else moved. The trap bar's and the squat's cards now name the week's phase in front of the sets — slow lowering, normal tempo, the three-second pause, fast — as the bench's always did.

**Why the look lives in Prep and the camp only keeps it.** Prep is the everyday program, so it's where the look gets built, with the food to match. A camp is for sharpening: it keeps about a third of the dose, which is roughly what holds muscle, and gives the time to the engine.

**Why there are no true maxes.** A one-rep max is a number for setting loads, and for a fighter it's nothing more. A top triple with one or two reps in reserve, read through the bar-speed profile, sets the same loads without the risk of failing a lift alone under the bar. Two heavy exposures in fourteen weeks — week 9 re-aims the heavy block, test week aims the camp — and neither one is a max.

**What this hands the camp.** Working weights ten percent higher and drawn from test week's top triples. An aerobic base built over five weeks and maintained over nine. Rounds already run at fight rest with a fade on the board. Tendons that have had fourteen weeks of holds and slow lowering. Hips and shoulders that pass the tests. A foundation block the camp can cut to a week. That's the difference between a camp that starts from where you are and one that starts from where you could be.
