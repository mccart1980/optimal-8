# IRON MIND v4.2 — MASTERY

*Runs inside Optimal 8 Fighter. One app, one day, one streak.*

**One page runs the day. Three curricula run the years: meditation, breath, hardship. Each has stages, gates and a ceiling, and each week you know exactly what's yours.**

The program whose only job is that your mind does what you tell it — attention that goes where you point it and stays, thoughts you watch instead of obey, a body that can be cold, empty, burning or gasping while the mind stays level, and a man who decides and acts instead of circling. Built from the oldest instructions there are on exactly this — the Buddha's, the Zen halls', the samurai's, the Stoics' — and from what the last twenty years of brain science has confirmed about why they work.

It costs 20–30 minutes a day, most of it sitting down, plus one hardship session a week that rides a session you already do. It attaches to the life you run: up before four, an hour in the gym, the scaffold, the shower, home.

## THE DAY — ONE PAGE, EVERY DAY

Your stage (Progression page) decides what goes in the three slots marked ★. Everything else is the same at every stage.

**ON WAKING · 2 minutes, before the phone**

1. **Three physiological sighs.** Full breath in through the nose, a second short sip on top, one long slow exhale through the mouth, longer than the inhale. Three times. The fastest reliable way to bring the body down, and you're rehearsing the rescue tool while calm so it's automatic under stress.
2. **The one thing.** Sixty seconds, in your head, four questions in order: *What is the one thing that, done today, makes the day a win?* → *See it done.* → *What's most likely to stop me?* → *When that happens, I will…* Marcus Aurelius did a version of this at dawn every day; the modern research on if-then planning shows it roughly doubles the odds a hard intention gets acted on.

**IN THE SESSION · free — written on the Optimal 8 Fighter session pages, run from that app**

Nose breathing and Ujjayi on everything easy; round-recovery breathing in every fight-sim rest; the 60-second settle after the last interval on Tuesday and Thursday; the post-max sit after Sunday's rounds; and this week's hardship test, wherever it rides. None of it is looked up here. It's on the page of the session it belongs to, and the app puts it in front of you at the moment it happens.

**ON SITE · free**

- **Nose breathing** while you work, at whatever pace lets you.
- **The reset**, at every break and before anything difficult: two sighs, then thirty seconds with your eyes on one fixed point and nothing else. Eyes steady, mind steadies — a hard-wired link, and why it works in thirty seconds.
- **★ The walking practice** on one break a day — your stage's version (walking meditation at Stage 1, walking breath holds from Stage 2, altitude walks from Stage 3).
- **One job at a time.** Start it, stay on it, finish it, then look up. *Zanshin* — the remaining mind.
- **Seven breaths.** Any decision you catch yourself circling: seven slow breaths, decide, act. Not instantly — that's impulse. Not endlessly — that's the circling. The samurai's rule from the *Hagakure*.
- **The phone rule.** First five minutes of any break, the phone stays in the pocket.

**LUNCH, IN THE VAN · 5–10 minutes**

3. **★ The breath practice** for your stage (Breath page). Skip it on a chaotic day; the floor covers you.

**THE SHOWER AFTER WORK**

4. **The cold slot** — whatever your hardship level prescribes, at the end of the shower, breath controlled throughout. Weekdays only in this slot; weekend rules on the Hardship page.

**EVENING · 15–35 minutes, after the mobility block**

5. **★ THE SIT** — your stage's length and content (Meditation page). This is the program; everything else supports it.
6. **THE REVIEW** — 2 minutes, spoken under your breath or in your head, never written. *Where did I go wrong today? What did I do well? What did I leave undone that I'll do tomorrow?* Facts, not adjectives; then it's over, no second pass. Seneca every night in bed; the Pythagoreans before him; the samurai's daily self-examination.

**SUNDAY · +15 minutes**

- The long sit, the last three minutes on death (Day's Work page).
- **BOLT score** on waking, before the sighs: one normal breath in through the nose, one normal breath out, pinch the nose, time to the *first definite urge* to breathe — not a grit hold. Gasp on release and it didn't count.
- **The four numbers**, ten seconds each: days done this week (of 7) · best clean cycles in a sit · BOLT · this week's Crossover gap.

**THE FLOOR · 4 minutes**

Overtime, someone ill, everything sliding — this still happens: three sighs → three minutes of box breathing → the review. **A floor day counts. A skipped day doesn't.** You are never training the practice; you are training the not-quitting. And **never miss twice.** One missed day is life. Two in a row is a decision, and you don't make that one.

## MEDITATION — THE PATH TO MASTERY

**What you're training.** Three things, in this order: *concentration* — holding one object until the mind goes still; *insight* — watching thoughts, sensations and moods as events you're observing rather than truths you're inside; and *transfer* — keeping either running while the body is under load. Every tradition that produced masters built them in that order, and the brain science agrees: concentration first quiets the chatter network that replays and worries; insight practice then changes your relationship to what that network produces; and only a mind that can do both on a chair can do either in round five.

**THE CATCH IS THE REP, NOT THE STILLNESS.** You're doing it right every time you notice you've drifted. Drifting is the set-up; catching is the lift. Measure the catch.

**How to sit.** Upright, chair or cushion, hands in the lap, eyes closed or half-open on the floor a metre ahead. Back straight, not stiff. Timer set so the clock isn't a thought. Nose breathing, natural — watch it, don't drive it.

### STAGE 1 · WEEKS 1–4 · CONCENTRATION · the sit is 12 minutes

**Breath counting, every evening.** Count each exhale silently, one to ten, then start again at one. Lose the count or catch yourself elsewhere — straight back to one, no punishment, no comment. A **clean cycle** is one to ten with no drift. **Track clean cycles, not minutes: 3 → 5 → 10 → 20.** Twenty consecutive clean cycles is properly hard and impossible to fake.

**Body scan, twice a week, in place of the count, lying down.** Attention travels foot to head, 10–15 seconds per area — sole, ankle, shin, knee — noticing whatever is there, warmth, pressure, nothing, without changing anything. This is how you learn to read your body accurately, which is the exact skill that stops arousal being misread as threat.

**Walking meditation, one site break a day, 3–5 minutes.** Walk at half pace. Attention on the feet: the lift, the swing, the place. Lose it, return to the feet. The Zen halls walk between every sit for a reason — it's the first transfer: attention that survives movement.

**Target:** 28 days done. Three clean cycles. 12-minute sits without physical restlessness.

### STAGE 2 · WEEKS 5–12 · INSIGHT · the sit is 15 minutes, 20 from week 9

**Count, then follow.** First half counting. Second half, drop the numbers and follow the breath: cool at the nostrils in, warm out, the pause at the bottom. As it settles the breath gets thinner and quieter — that's correct; don't deepen it to feel it, sharpen attention instead.

**Noting — the crucial skill.** When something pulls you off the breath, label it silently, one word, by category — *hearing · feeling · thinking · planning · remembering* — and return. Not "worrying about the job" (still a story you're inside); "thinking" (a category of event you're watching). This single refinement is the mechanism of thought control: a thought becomes something happening, not something true. Brain imaging shows labelling turns the alarm centre down. Name it to tame it.

**Goodwill, Sunday, the last five minutes of the long sit.** Silently, in sequence: *may I be well* → someone you love → someone neutral, the man at the yard → someone difficult. It sounds soft and it is the hardest thing on this page, because it is the direct antidote to being provoked. The test isn't a feeling. **Did anything move you this week, and did you notice before or after you reacted?** That's *fudōshin* — the immovable mind — measured honestly.

**Candle gazing, twice a week, 5 minutes before the sit.** Dim room, candle at arm's length, eye level. Gaze without blinking as long as comfortable — 30–60 seconds to start — then close the eyes and hold the after-image until it fades, then keep the eyes closed and hold the empty dark for thirty seconds more. That last phase is the bridge from concentration to open awareness. 3–5 rounds. Watery eyes are normal; strain isn't. Skip with any eye condition.

**Rehearsal, once a week, real time.** Pick one specific hard thing coming this week — a round, a max, a conversation — and run it through your own eyes, in real time (a round takes three minutes to rehearse, not twenty seconds), with the nerves included. Then the adversity run: the moment it goes wrong — gassing mid-round, caught clean, the mind saying quit — watched, and answered with control. You're pre-building the response so the ambush has already been met. Also the **pre-fight 90 seconds**, in the car before any sparring: two sighs; one line, second person, your own name — *"You've done harder. Sharp and relaxed."*; the single concrete thing you'll do when the first bell goes. Then in.

**Focus under load begins.** Once a week, meditation-quality attention held during hard effort — a wall sit to failure with the breath as anchor. Log where attention broke and where the body broke. That's the Crossover gap, and it's now yours to close (Hardship page).

**Target:** 5 clean cycles. Noting automatic. 20-minute sits. The breath thinning without alarm.

### STAGE 3 · WEEKS 13–24 · OPEN · the sit is 20 minutes, 30 on Sunday

**Count, follow, open.** Five minutes counting, ten following, then the anchor is dropped. Whatever arises — a sound, a thought, an itch, a mood — arrives, is seen clearly, and passes, without following it and without pushing it away. Where counting trains holding, this trains watching without grabbing. The Zen name for what it produces is *mushin* — no-mind: not emptiness, but a mind that doesn't stop on anything. The instant attention fixes on one thing, you've lost everything else — in the ring, that's the shot you didn't see.

**The four-station switch drill, 5 minutes, three times a week, before the sit.** Attention has width (broad or narrow) and direction (outward or inward) — four states — and mastery is switching cleanly on demand, not holding one. Stuck narrow-outward: fixating on the shot that landed and eating the one behind it. Stuck broad-inward: analysing mid-round. Stuck narrow-inward: every twinge enormous — the panic state, the aperture jammed shut on your own body. The drill, 30 seconds per station, three rounds, on a timer: **(1)** the whole room at once, soft eyes → **(2)** one mark on the wall, nothing else exists → **(3)** the whole body as one sensation → **(4)** the air at the nostrils only. Progress 30s → 20s → 10s per station; then run it during a wall sit; then during conditioning. **The training effect is in the switch.**

**Moving focus — *zanshin* proper.** Slow-motion shadow boxing, ten minutes, once a week, where the target isn't the movement but the state: total presence, nothing left over for stray thought, awareness continuing after each action finishes. Your coach owns boxing; this is your mind, using your body as the object.

**Focus under load, weekly, now on the harder tests** (Hardship page).

**What advanced feels like — so you know when you're there.** Around this stage, in a good sit, the breath becomes a thin thread you'd lose if you looked away; sounds arrive and leave without a ripple; the body feels light or seems to fade; there's a quiet brightness behind the eyes and, at times, a plain physical pleasure in simply sitting. The old texts call this *access concentration* — the doorway. It isn't the goal and it isn't chased; it's the sign that the mind has stopped fighting itself. When it comes, do nothing different. When it doesn't, do nothing different.

**Target:** 10 clean cycles. 30-minute sits. Open awareness stable for the last five minutes. Switch drill at 10 seconds a station.

### STAGE 4 · WEEK 25 ONWARD · MASTERY · sits of 30–60 minutes

**Just sitting — *shikantaza*.** No anchor, no technique, no count, no goal. Upright, alert, present with whatever is. It sounds like nothing; it's the hardest practice there is, because there's nothing to hold and nothing to achieve, and every trick the mind has for getting somewhere is exposed. Ten minutes at the end of the sit, growing.

**Extended sits.** 30 → 45 → 60 minutes, one long sit a week, the rest at 20–30. From here, progression is duration and consistency, not technique.

**The half-day sit, twice a year.** Three to four hours, sitting and walking alternately, no phone, no talking, no reading. Nothing else will show you your own mind as clearly. **The full day, once a year** if the half-day holds. A retreat with a teacher, if you ever want one, is the fastest way up from here and the only thing this document can't give you.

**Beyond.** The classical maps — the Buddha's absorptions (*jhāna*), the Zen *samādhi* — describe states of concentration deeper than access, in which the mind is completely gathered and the ordinary sense of effort disappears. They are real, they are reachable by exactly the road you're on, and they're not required for anything on these pages. If they arrive, a teacher is worth finding. If they don't, twenty clean cycles and a sixty-minute sit is mastery by any standard that matters on a scaffold or in a ring.

**Yoga Nidra — recovery, any stage,** 20–45 minutes lying down when you need resetting: settle two minutes → one short present-tense intention, as if already true → rapid rotation of attention around the body, naming each part silently, 1–2 seconds each, too fast to dwell → count breaths backward from 27, pick up wherever you land → pairs of opposites, heavy then light, warm then cool → a few rapid images, no story → return to the intention with more conviction → come back slowly.

## THE BREATH — FOUR STAGES

Two facts decide this page. First: the exhale is the brake. The heart slows a little on every out-breath and speeds on every in-breath, so a long exhale is a direct hand on the brake. Second: panic is largely a false alarm about carbon dioxide — the body reads rising CO2 as suffocation. The fix is not less CO2 but a nervous system that has learned, gently and repeatedly, that air hunger is safe. So the road runs: calm → tolerate → control → power. **In that order, with the gates cleared honestly. The heavy tools are earned, and they're earned by the slow ones.**

**Safety, at every stage.** Breath holds and any fast breathing: seated or lying, never in or near water, never driving, never standing. Never holds and cold together. If anything tips toward panic: stop, sigh, session over — that's the tool used correctly, not a failure. Forceful techniques carry real contraindications — high blood pressure, heart conditions, epilepsy, recent abdominal surgery, severe asthma; if any ever apply, those techniques are out and the rest still works.

### STAGE 1 · WEEKS 1–4 · CALM

**The physiological sigh — the rescue tool, owned for life.** Two inhales through the nose — a full one, a short top-up — one long exhale through the mouth. One to three brings you down in about eight seconds. On waking, on every reset, between rounds, and the moment anything tips toward panic. In a head-to-head trial, five minutes a day of these on repeat beat box breathing and meditation for mood and resting breathing rate over a month — so on any day you want more calm than the routine gives, five minutes of continuous sighing is the best-evidenced five minutes there is.

**Box breathing — lunch, 5 minutes.** In through the nose 4 → hold 4 → out 4 → hold empty 4. If 4 strains, 3. The one the SEALs are taught because it works sitting in anything.

**Coherent breathing — any evening, 5–10 minutes, before bed if sleep is poor.** In 4, out 6, nose only — about six breaths a minute, the rate at which heart rhythm and breath lock together and the calming branch runs strongest.

**Nose breathing, all day.** Walking, working, training at any pace you can hold it. The single biggest lever on your BOLT.

**Diaphragm check, three evenings a week, 5 minutes.** Lie down, one hand on the chest, one on the belly. Only the belly hand moves. If you can't do it lying down, you can't do it under stress.

**Walking practice on site:** Stage 1 is walking meditation (Meditation page).

**Gate → Stage 2:** BOLT 20 seconds. Nasal automatic in easy training.

### STAGE 2 · WEEKS 5–12 · TOLERATE

**Breathe Light — lunch, 10 minutes, daily; a second round in the evening when you can.** Sit upright. Nose only. Breathe deliberately *lighter and quieter* than you want to — slightly less air than feels comfortable — until there's a mild, tolerable air hunger you could hold for minutes without the shoulders lifting or the breath going ragged. Gasping or chaotic means too light; ease off. This resets the CO2 alarm and moves your BOLT more than everything else combined. It is gentle on purpose.

**The CO2 table — twice a week, Sunday afternoon and Thursday lunch.** Seated, dry, never after cardio, nose-only rests, **no big breathing between rounds** — that defeats the point. Set the hold at 50% of your comfortable max. Eight rounds; the hold never changes, the rest shrinks: 2:00 → 1:45 → 1:30 → 1:15 → 1:00 → 0:45 → 0:30 → 0:15. By round six it's unpleasant. That unpleasantness is CO2 accumulating, and tolerating it calmly is the training.

**Walking breath holds — the site walking practice.** Nose. Exhale normally, pinch, walk 10–20 paces, release, recover through the nose for a full minute. 5–8 reps. Gentle: recovered within 2–3 breaths or it was too far.

**Alternate-nostril breathing — three evenings a week, 5 minutes, no holds yet.** Thumb closes the right nostril, in through the left for 4; close the left with the ring finger, out through the right for 6; in through the right 4; close, out through the left 6. That's one round. Slow, quiet, even. It's the classical settling breath before meditation, and it gives the counting mind something to do.

**Ujjayi on every easy ride. Round-recovery breathing after every hard interval.** Both taught on the Day page.

**Gate → Stage 3:** BOLT 25s+. Breathe Light clean for 10 minutes. No panic response to mild air hunger.

### STAGE 3 · WEEKS 13–24 · CONTROL

**The classical ratio — three evenings a week, 5–10 minutes, replacing the plain alternate-nostril.** In 4 · hold 8 · out 8, nose only, built slowly toward in 4 · hold 16 · out 8 over months. This is *kumbhaka* — the held breath the yogis built their whole system on. Any gasping on the exit means the ratio is too aggressive; drop back.

**Humming breath (*bhramari*) — any evening, 2–3 minutes.** In through the nose, then a long, low hum on the exhale with the mouth closed, until the breath runs out. Ten to fifteen rounds. It lengthens the exhale automatically, the vibration settles the nervous system, and the hum raises nitric oxide in the nasal passages. The best pre-sleep breath there is.

**The O2 table — once a week, Saturday late afternoon, four-plus hours after the session, never the same day as a CO2 table.** Rest stays fixed at 2:00, the hold grows: rounds 1–8 at 50 → 55 → 60 → 65 → 70 → 75 → 80 → 85% of your max. Seated, dry. Tingling hands or lips, or any change in vision — stop the table entirely. That's a signal, not a badge.

**Altitude walks — the site walking practice, 2–3 times a week.** Steady nasal walking; exhale, pinch, walk holding to a strong-but-not-maximal air hunger (6–7 out of 10), release, recover fully for a minute. 5 reps. Paces per hold climbing over the weeks is the number.

**★ Sensation exposure — the bridge to Stage 4. Twice a week, seated, calm day, nothing stressful ahead.** Thirty seconds of deliberate fast, deep breathing through the mouth — then stop, two sighs, and five minutes of Breathe Light. That's the whole thing. It will produce tingling, lightness, a quick heart, a tight chest — the sensations the alarm is tuned to. You are teaching the alarm, in thirty controlled seconds at a time, that these sensations are safe and boring. This is the same method panic clinics use, because it's the one that works: not avoiding the sensations, and not being ambushed by them — meeting them on purpose, small, with the brakes in your hand. Progress 30 → 45 → 60 seconds over eight weeks. **The gate to Stage 4 is boredom:** when sixty seconds produces sensation and nothing else — no urge to stop, no story — the heavy tools are yours.

**Punch-breath integration — trains itself on every landmine punch, throw and bag round.** Sharp exhale on impact, nose or pursed lips, never an open mouth — the open mouth loses the jaw and the brace. Never hold the breath through a combination: inhale, hold, unload is four seconds of no gas exchange exactly when demand peaks, and it's why men gas in round two. Breathe in the gaps: after the combination, on the reset, on the move.

**Gate → Stage 4:** BOLT 35s+. Sensation exposure at 60 seconds producing boredom. Stage 3 stable for eight-plus weeks.

### STAGE 4 · WEEK 25 ONWARD · POWER — the heavy tools, used sparingly

These are the tools you were drawn to at the start. They're here, with the brakes you built. Every one of them is a stimulant: it produces the alert, charged, tingling state by driving CO2 down hard. Used on a base of six months of calm and tolerance, that state is yours to visit. Used without it, it's the panic physiology with a name on it.

**Wim Hof rounds — up to three times a week, never two days running.** Seated or lying, calm day. Entry protocol: 20 full breaths — deep in through the nose or mouth, let the exhale fall out — then after the last, exhale and hold empty as long as comfortable; one full breath in, hold 15 seconds, release. That's a round. Two rounds to start. Build to 30–40 breaths and three rounds only if it lands as alert-calm, never agitated. **Always finish with five minutes of Breathe Light** to re-sensitise; skip that and you've hyperventilated twice a day and called it training. Never before water, never before driving, never standing.

**Empty-lung holds (FRC) — once a week, 5 rounds, 2 minutes rest, seated.** Hold on a relaxed *exhale*, not a full inhale. Air hunger arrives fast and hard at low total time; it isolates the stay-calm-while-the-body-screams response almost completely. The purest drill for your exact goal.

**Breath-hold repeats under effort — once a week, replacing the altitude walk.** Exhale, hold, jog or hard-walk 20–40 paces, release, full nasal recovery. 5–8 reps. Brutal, effective, only on a built base.

**Kapalabhati — optional, 2–3 rounds of 20–30 pumps.** Sharp belly exhales, passive inhales; then a normal breath and a comfortable hold. Alert and clarifying. **Bhastrika** — bellows, both directions forceful — only after Kapalabhati is easy, same contraindications, more intense.

**Extended kumbhaka** — the ratio grows toward in 8 · hold 32 · out 16 over years, not months. A qualified teacher helps here more than anywhere.

**Holotropic** — only with a trained facilitator, never solo, never near water, and retired permanently the first time it tips toward panic. Everything it offers, the tools above deliver with control.

## HARDSHIP — THE LADDER AND THE TESTS

*Thriving in hardship is not enduring it. It's staying yourself inside it.*

**CARDINAL RULE: controlled breath throughout, always.** Discomfort without breath control is just suffering. Discomfort with breath control is the training.

**THE FIRST ARROW AND THE SECOND — the operating teaching.** When something hard hits — cold on skin, air hunger, burning quads, round five with heavy hands — two arrows land. The first is the event. The second is the reaction: *get me out, I can't, how long left.* The second arrow hurts more than the first, and the second one is optional. **The tell: the second arrow is always a sentence. The first never is.** Pure sensation has no words. The moment you find words, you've found the second arrow, and you can put it down. Run this separation silently for sixty seconds inside every hardship session. Noting on a cushion is rehearsal; noting in the cold is the performance.

### THE LADDER

**LEVEL 1 · weeks 1–8 · 3 sessions a week**
- Cold: 30–60 seconds at the end of the after-work shower, three weekdays. Breath long and slow on the way in; the gasp is the first arrow — one long exhale, and you stay.
- One static hold to failure a week — wall sit or plank — with attention on the breath and the drift count logged.
- Twenty minutes of silence a week: one session with no music — Wednesday's sled block or Sunday's throws does it.
- One hardship test a week from the rotation below.
**Gate: eight consecutive weeks, none missed.**

**LEVEL 2 · weeks 9–16 · 4 sessions a week**
- Cold: 2–3 minutes, four weekdays, and the water as cold as the shower goes.
- The hold to failure with the countback (test 3, below).
- Forty-five minutes of silence a week: two sessions with no music.
- **The hungry hour:** once a week, the last hour before the 5pm feed is worked through without the 3pm shake — the hunger treated as a first arrow, named, and put down. Never on a Tuesday or Friday (those feeds load the next morning's session).
**Gate: eight weeks · the cold no longer produces a gasp.**

**LEVEL 3 · week 17 onward · 4–5 sessions a week**
- Cold: 3–5 minutes — a plunge or a cold bath if you can get one, stacked cold showers if not — with the anchor running the whole time.
- Heat: sauna 15–20 minutes, once or twice a week if available, breath slow and nasal, sitting still — the same practice at the other end of the thermometer. Never straight into cold from heat before a training day.
- No-music training as the default, not the exception. Sixty to ninety minutes of silence a week.
- Combined stressors: cold with the four-station drill running; the wall sit with Breathe Light.
**Gate: twelve weeks, plus a completed Hell Week.**

**THE CEILING** — Hell Week two or three times a year (the training app's IRON switch runs it, in a week without a max or a test day), one deliberate comfort-deprivation day a quarter — no phone, no music, plain food, cold water, the floor slept on — and the solo half-day sit. **Visit the ceiling. Don't live there.**

### THE FIVE TESTS — one a week, rotating, mind and body scored together

Each **replaces** a finisher or rides an existing session — never added on top. Score each: where the mind quit and where the body quit. **The gap between them is the Crossover gap — the only number that proves any of this transferred.** Your physical numbers climb first. The mental numbers are what you're here for, and they lag by months. That lag is the program.

1. **THE SILENT SLED.** Wednesday's sled block (it's written on that page), no music, counting exhales. Log the exhale where attention broke and the run where the legs broke.
2. **THE POST-MAX SIT.** Sunday, straight after the fight sim (it's on that page — three minutes there, five when it's the week's test): sit, eyes closed, heart at 170-plus, chest heaving — find the breath. Log seconds to settle onto the anchor. **This is the corner between rounds, trained. Nobody practises it and everybody needs it.**
3. **HELD POSITION UNDER COGNITIVE LOAD.** Wall sit or plank to failure while counting backward from 300 in sevens. Log hold time and the number reached; compare with your plain hold. A 15–25% drop is normal; a smaller gap means you're dual-capable rather than needing every scrap of attention just to hang on.
4. **THE NASAL THRESHOLD.** Thursday's bike, weeks 4, 9 and 14, or any Thursday you make it the week's test: eight minutes nose only, raising the pace every two minutes until the mouth has to open; then the interval session runs one round short. Log the pace — and the honest half: did the mouth open because it had to, **or because you caved?** That answer is worth more than the number.
5. **COLD WITH AN ANCHOR.** Cold at your level, anchor running throughout, count the drifts. The cold is the first arrow; **every drift you count was a sentence in your head, not a sensation on your skin.**

### THE COLLISION RULES — precedence, fixed

1. **Optimal 8 Fighter owns your body's budget.** Iron Mind attaches to what remains; its only standalone claims are seated minutes and the shower.
2. **Cold:** weekdays in the after-work shower. Never within the hour before training. Weekends, not within four hours after the session — cold straight after lifting blunts the adaptation you just paid for — so Saturday and Sunday cold is evening cold. Hell Week's cold finishes are fine; there's no lifting adaptation that week to blunt.
3. **Holds and cold never together.**
4. **Fasts:** none in a training week beyond the hungry hour. Extended fasts happen on Hell Week day six only — never in a training week; they fight the 5pm feeds that run your weekend.
5. **Taper weeks 15–16 of the training cycle: no maximal hardship sessions.** Breath, sits and the daily core continue; the tests pause.
6. **One hardship test per week. Never two.**

## THE THOUGHTS — CONTROL OF THE MIND

You cannot stop a thought arriving. Nobody can; that's not what control is. Control is what happens in the half-second after it arrives, and that half-second is trainable. The sit trains it every night. These are the tools for the day.

**THE FIVE — the Buddha's instructions for an unwanted thought, in order.** (The *Vitakkasanthana Sutta*, the discourse on the removal of distracting thoughts. Twenty-five centuries old; modern psychology has re-discovered every step.)

1. **Replace it.** Turn attention to something else worth attending to — the breath, the job in your hands, the one thing. A carpenter knocking out a coarse peg with a fine one. Most thoughts die here.
2. **Look at the cost.** If it won't go, look straight at what it's doing: *this thought is costing me the next hour and giving nothing back.* Seen clearly, a useless thought loses its grip.
3. **Turn away.** Don't feed it, don't fight it — take your eyes off it. Fighting a thought gives it your attention; ignoring it starves it.
4. **Slow it down.** Notice the thought is running and ask what's driving it. The Buddha's image: a man walking fast who thinks *why am I walking fast?* — and walks. Then *why am I walking?* — and stands. Then sits, then lies down. The thinking slows because you've stopped pushing it.
5. **Force it.** Only when all else fails: teeth together, tongue to the roof of the mouth, and crush the thought with will. The last resort — but it exists, and knowing it exists is itself control.

**NAMING.** The same skill you train in the sit, used in the day. When a thought has hold of you, name it in one word by category — *thinking · remembering · planning · worrying* — and it becomes a thing you're looking at instead of a place you're standing.

**WHAT IS YOURS.** Epictetus opened his handbook with the only division that matters: some things are up to you — your attention, your judgements, your actions — and everything else isn't: other people, the weather, the past, the outcome. Control means spending yourself entirely on the first column and nothing on the second. Every time you catch yourself grinding on something in the second column, that's a thought for the Five.

**SELF-DISTANCED SELF-TALK.** *I can't do this* keeps you inside the experience. *You've done harder — two more lengths* puts you outside it, looking at it. Second person, your own name, short orders. It measurably cuts anxiety and holds up under fatigue, which is why it survives round five.

**THE REFRAME — with its one prohibition.** A racing heart before something hard is oxygen going where it's needed. Butterflies are mobilisation. Air hunger in Breathe Light is the alarm being retrained. The arousal of stress and the arousal of readiness are the same arousal; only the label differs, and the label is yours. **The one place this must never be used: anything tipping toward panic. Down-regulate first, always. The reframe is for arousal you chose, never for arousal that's running you.**

**THE STATES — named, so they're targets.** *Mushin* — no-mind: the mind that doesn't stop on anything; trained by open awareness. *Fudōshin* — the immovable mind: not rigid, just not moved — by provocation, a bad round, or a man trying to make you emotional; trained by goodwill, tested weekly. *Zanshin* — the remaining mind: awareness that continues after the action finishes; trained moving, and on the scaffold. *Read: Takuan Sōhō, "The Unfettered Mind" — a Zen priest's letters to a swordsman on exactly this.*

## THE DAY'S WORK — DEDICATION, PRODUCTIVITY, BEING PROACTIVE

**Dedication is not a feeling. It's a streak.** The days-done number is the only measure of it; the floor and never-miss-twice are how it's protected. Motivation gets you to day four; the streak gets you to day four hundred.

**Proactive means deciding in advance.** The one thing at dawn, the if-then for its obstacle, seven breaths for the decisions that come up on the day. A decision made in the morning costs nothing at four o'clock; a decision left until four o'clock costs everything. The samurai settled the matter beforehand so the moment didn't get a vote.

**Productive means one job, finished.** Not the plan for the job. Not three jobs a third done. *Zanshin*: start, stay, finish, then look up. The brain rewards a finished thing with a small hit of the chemistry that makes anything feel worth doing again, and it can be trained to reward the effort itself, not just the outcome. That's the switch between a man who does things when he feels like it and a man who feels like it because he does things.

**The evening review is where it lands.** Wrong · well · undone-and-tomorrow. The third answer becomes tomorrow's one thing. Decide at dawn, do, review at night, decide again — the engine of a directed life, and the practice of every serious tradition because it's the only thing that works.

**SUNDAY: THREE MINUTES ON DEATH.** The last three minutes of the Sunday sit. The *Hagakure* says meditation on inevitable death should be performed daily; the Stoics kept a reminder of it in view; the Buddha called mindfulness of death the practice that, developed, bears the greatest fruit. It is not morbid; it's the fastest sharpening of priorities there is. Sit with the plain fact that the days are numbered and unknown, then one question: *what would I stop putting off if this were the last month?* The honest answer goes in Monday's one thing. Three minutes, then open the eyes and get on with the week.

**THE CODE.** *Yu* — courage: acting while the fear is still there. *Gi* — the right hard thing because it's right. *Makoto* — total honesty with yourself: no "I'll start tomorrow", no "that doesn't count". And the Spartan overlay: when something is hard, you finish it. Not try. Finish.

## THE COACHING NOTE

**The sit is the program.** Every evening, for the rest of your life. Everything else exists to make that happen and to carry it into the day, the cold, and the ring.

**Measure the catch, not the stillness.** The mind wanders; that isn't a defect. The speed of the catch is what's trainable — on the chair, on the scaffold, in round five.

**Earn the heavy tools.** The cold, the holds, the fast breathing — they're all here, and every one of them is worth more on the base the slow work builds. Six months of calm buys you the rest of your life with the stimulants in your hand instead of the other way round.

**Never miss twice.** The floor on a bad day, the review even when there's nothing to say. Dedication isn't a quality you have. It's a streak you keep.
